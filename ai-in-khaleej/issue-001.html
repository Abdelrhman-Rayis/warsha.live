The agent moment has arrived

01. What Gulf faculty should do this semester

The practical shift in higher education is no longer just “chatting with AI.” It is “placing AI inside real academic work.” OpenAI is pitching ChatGPT Edu as a campus-wide layer for students, faculty, researchers, and administrators, and has now extended that logic into workspace agents that can run recurring workflows across tools with approvals and admin controls. Anthropic’s education offer is centered on Projects and Learning mode, which frames AI as a bounded academic workspace rather than a one-off conversation. Google is doing the same from a source-grounded angle with NotebookLM and assignable Gems and notebooks in Classroom.

For Gulf universities, that matters for three reasons. First, faculty are the people who actually decide whether AI becomes academically useful or administratively chaotic. Second, Arabic-medium and bilingual teaching adds a real quality-control layer that generic US-centered newsletters usually ignore. Third, the governance question is now immediate: once AI can touch files, notes, messages, timetables, or student-facing workflows, the conversation moves from “tool choice” to “institutional responsibility.”

A pedagogy pattern worth stealing

The most promising course pattern right now is the course-specific GPT or course-specific project notebook. The idea is simple: create one bounded assistant that knows your syllabus, your readings, your rubric, your assignment instructions, and a few high-quality examples of the kinds of feedback you already give. Then tell it clearly what it must never do. OpenAI explicitly recommends course- and task-specific GPTs for university workspaces, and OpenAI’s own higher-ed agent guidance stresses that good assistants start with one clear job, trusted knowledge sources, clear steps, review points, and an owner. Anthropic’s Projects are built for the same pattern: self-contained workspaces with uploaded context and project-specific instructions.

This works best when the assistant is not framed as “write for the student,” but as “coach the student through course thinking.” Anthropic’s Learning mode is especially explicit here: guide rather than answer, use Socratic questioning, emphasize core concepts, and provide useful templates instead of shortcuts. That is the right pedagogic frame for Gulf faculty as well, especially in programs where plagiarism anxiety would otherwise kill adoption before it starts.

A workable five-step recipe is:

  • Pick one course and one repeated friction point. Do not begin with “AI for my whole teaching load.” Begin with one bounded job such as weekly reading checks, draft outline coaching, lab report troubleshooting, or exam revision prompts. OpenAI’s own higher-ed guidance repeatedly recommends starting with one job because broad agents drift and are harder to trust.
  • Upload only trusted materials: syllabus, readings, rubric, model answers, sample comments, and department-approved writing conventions.
  • Write behavioral rules before anything else: what the assistant should produce, what it must refuse, and when it should ask the student to go back to the source text.
  • Pilot with a small group first, then collect failure modes: unsupported claims, over-helpful drafting, weak citation, terminology drift, or Arabic transliteration errors.
  • Review and revise weekly. A course-specific assistant is not a static asset. It is a teaching artifact.

Starter system prompt. Copy, paste, edit the bracketed parts:

You are a study partner for [COURSE NAME], taught by [INSTRUCTOR NAME] at [UNIVERSITY]. Your job is to help students [PICK ONE: understand difficult concepts / improve their writing / practice problem-solving / prepare for exams]. You speak in a [warm but rigorous / formal / Socratic] register. Hard rules: • Never write a complete assignment for a student. If asked, redirect them to think through the next step. • Always cite which course material your answer is grounded in. If the answer is not in the course material, say so explicitly. • When a student is wrong, do not just give the right answer. Ask them what reasoning led them there, then guide them toward the correct frame. • If a student asks something outside the scope of this course, suggest they raise it in office hours. Voice: match the teaching style in the uploaded materials. When uncertain, lean toward the questioning style modeled in the example feedback you have been trained on. Language: respond in the language the student writes to you in. For Arabic-language responses, use Modern Standard Arabic unless the student writes in dialect, in which case match their register.

Arabic caveat

For Arabic-medium or bilingual courses, create a one-page terminology sheet and upload it alongside the syllabus. Use Modern Standard Arabic for core explanations unless the course explicitly wants dialectal examples. Check any scanned Arabic PDFs before using them as source material: Google documents real source and citation limits in NotebookLM, and Arabic OCR research still treats cursive script, diacritics, and typography as persistent technical challenges.

02. A tool worth your time: NotebookLM

What it is. Google's research notebook tool. You upload sources (PDFs, articles, slides, your own notes) and ask questions. It answers only from those sources, with citations to the exact passage. There is also an "audio overview" feature that turns your sources into a podcast-style discussion between two synthetic voices.

Why does it earn its place? NotebookLM is the first AI tool I have tested that is built for the researcher's workflow rather than the writer's. It refuses to invent. It cites. It is faster than re-reading. For literature reviews, dissertation supervision, and grant prep, the ROI is high. Google also states that NotebookLM now supports 80+ languages, including Arabic output, and that for Google Workspace for Education, it is a core service rather than a side experiment.

A video pick for this week

Video pick: Watch the first 20 minutes of OpenAI Forum’s Harvard’s AI-Enhanced Classroom: Revolutionizing Learning with Custom GPTs. The full session runs 1:13:03, but the opening 20 minutes are enough to justify the time.

Three takeaways matter.

  • AI adoption in universities happens in layers. OpenAI’s education team frames the journey as individual use, then team-level workflows, then organization-wide AI touchpoints. That is a much better framing for chairs and CTLs than “ban or allow ChatGPT.”
  • Course-specific GPTs matter when they are tied to course knowledge. The session explicitly provides examples, such as orientation GPTs and class-specific assistants, that help students ask questions grounded in the program's actual knowledge.
  • Access and cost are still real adoption constraints. The Q&A openly raises the challenge that custom GPT collaboration is stronger inside paid workspaces such as ChatGPT Edu or Teams, while the free product has limits. That is exactly why this newsletter focuses on bounded pedagogy patterns and institution-safe trial designs, rather than the most advanced premium features.

Watch if you teach… large-enrolment courses, writing-heavy modules, business and policy subjects, research methods, or any course where students repeatedly need help interpreting course-specific material rather than just “finding an answer.”
